Case study · Personal venture

SendMyNotes

A thoughtful card, without the stationery drawer or mailbox trip.

SendMyNotes explores how to make sending a personal paper card easier while keeping the message, design choice and recipient experience at the heart of the gesture.

Live product and paid-channel experiment · Customer demand still being validated

Back to selected ventures Read the DocketStamp case study →

01 · The problem

Keep the gesture. Remove the errands.

A physical card can feel more considered than a quick message, but sending one takes several small steps: find a card, write the note, address it, add postage and get it to the mailbox. SendMyNotes is a product bet that phone-based convenience can remove that friction without making the note feel generic.

02 · Product decisions

Treat sender ease and recipient feeling as one journey.

  • Keep the message personal. The sender chooses the design and writes the words that will go inside the card.
  • Be accurate about production. The interior message is written by automated equipment holding real pens, then the card is mailed.
  • Connect the two brand roles. SendMyNotes is the sender-facing service; Aster & Blanche is the recipient-facing stationery identity. They describe different sides of one card journey.

03 · My role

Own the product question from promise to fulfillment.

I shape the product direction, choose what to test and work directly across experience, software and operations. I keep the message, card, price, checkout and mailing handoff in view together, because the promise only works when the whole path feels coherent.

04 · Delivery approach

Use agents for bounded work; keep the product call human.

I split implementation into focused tasks and use agents to research, build and critique pieces of the experience. I then integrate the real changes, check the behavior that matters and own the release decision.

  1. Frame the user promise. Make it easy to send without taking the personal words out of the sender’s hands.
  2. Set clear boundaries. Keep product language grounded in what the fulfillment path actually supports.
  3. Verify behavior before release. A discount-flow fix tied the selected checkout total to the payment intent; behavioral regressions and a staged release covered price changes and retries.

05 · Current evidence

The product is still learning whether the promise resonates.

I have no verified independent customer sale to report, and a founder test does not count as customer evidence.

06 · Lesson and next question

Convenience is only valuable if the card still feels personal.

The next question is whether a simpler sender path helps more people turn an intention into a physical gesture—while the message and card still feel considered to the person receiving them. Answering that calls for trustworthy evidence from real completed orders, not assumptions about interest.
